Technology overview

01 — data pipeline

Every hotspot starts as raw, messy signal

From more than 700 km up, a thermal satellite can tell that a patch of ground is hot, but not why. A refinery flare burning exactly as designed, a farmer clearing stubble and a ruptured pipeline can all show up as the same bright pixel, anywhere from 375 m to a kilometre across.

So VahniX never judges a pixel on its own. Each detection is stacked with four more layers: a sharper thermal reading, the condition of the ground, what is built there, and how that exact spot has behaved for the past year. Every layer is then pinned to the same hexagonal H3 cell, so they all describe one patch of ground at one moment.

What comes out is 38 numbers per detection. Together they say how hot it is, what it sits next to, whether the surface is scorched and how normal this is for this place, which is what lets the model separate a routine flare from an emergency.

Each source answers a different question about the same patch of ground, and each fills a fixed share of the 38 numbers the model reads for every detection.

02 — thermal fingerprint

Every hotspot carries its own fingerprint

A satellite detection is a snapshot. It says a pixel was hot during one pass, not whether it is hot every afternoon. Refinery flares, steel furnaces, cement kilns and brick kilns run at 1,000–2,000 K all year, so they trip the standard fire tests on every clear pass. In heavy industrial corridors, over 85% of thermal detections are normal process heat.

So VahniX gives every H3 cell a memory. It records how often the cell has run hot over 30, 90 and 365 days, whether it burns as strongly at night as by day, how steady its power is, and what it sits next to. Together these make a thermal fingerprint: stable for a working flare, and very different for a crop fire, a wildfire or a rupture.

Each new detection is checked against that fingerprint instead of a generic threshold. A flare at its usual power is logged and its baseline updated. The same flare suddenly running 3.5 times hotter than its own normal is escalated as an emergency.

Six ways a hotspot can behave

Temperature alone can't separate these. What separates them is behaviour over time and place, and that is what the fingerprint records. Each strip shows an illustrative 14 days of satellite passes: day on top, night below.

One hotspot, one full answer

When a cell is resolved, the portal hands over everything behind the call rather than a label and a colour.

  • What it is, and how surethe class and the model's confidence, beside a severity score built from the hazard, not from the heat alone.
  • The measurements that decided itthe power surge against this cell's own baseline, the burn scar from Sentinel-2, the distance to the nearest mapped asset.
  • Why, in plain wordsthe reasons an operator can act on and repeat to someone else, each one traceable back to a feature in the vector.
  • A way further inevery card opens into the full 38-dimension audit for that coordinate, so no conclusion has to be taken on trust.

The same card is produced for every detection the pipeline resolves, whether it turns out to be a refinery flare, a field fire or sensor glint. None of them are summarised away.

One detection, one full audit

Because every anomaly reads differently, the portal never reduces a detection to a single label. Each one opens into its full audit: all 38 measured dimensions on the left, and on the right the exact Shapley contribution each one made to the classification, summing to the model output.

When a fingerprint breaks

Every cell is judged against its own history, not a national threshold.

  • SurgeFRP above 3.5 times the cell's 90-day baseline sends even a known flare into the emergency protocol.
  • Anomalya z-score above 5 against the 90-day mean and spread marks a statistical break.
  • One incident, not twentyif an alert is already open for that spot within the last 60 minutes, the new pass joins it instead of opening another.
  • Severity decides who hears firstmoderate breaches post to the regional disaster portal, severe hazmat events send SMS and webhooks to plant safety officers, and catastrophic events trigger CAP alerts for NDRF and defence dispatch. All of them push live to the dashboard.

Hypothetical example from the spec: a refinery cell with a 132 MW baseline reads 1,850 MW and Sentinel-2 shows a fresh burn scar. It is classified as an industrial emergency at 99.1% and raised as a P1 incident, while the twelve routine flares in the same complex stay within their baselines.

03 — xplainability

The X stands for a reason

A probability is not evidence. No control room mobilises NDRF, or wakes a plant safety officer at 3 a.m., because a model printed 99%. The officer needs to know what the model saw, and needs it in the same breath as the alert.

So every classification ships with an exact breakdown of what moved it. TreeSHAP splits the prediction into one number per feature, in log-odds, and those numbers are complete by construction: they add up to precisely the distance between this incident and the average case. Nothing is left in a black box.

It costs under 3.5 ms, which is why it runs on every detection rather than on request. The same vector is then translated three ways: into the evidence card an officer reads, into plain-language answers from the assistant, and into a signed audit record for the review afterwards.

The recurrence that protects the flare from a false alarm is the same feature that, once it is absent, helps convict the rupture. No single number decides either case.

Where those numbers come from

SHAP values are Shapley values from cooperative game theory, applied to features instead of players. Each feature is paid what it adds, averaged over every order the features could arrive in.

The value of a feature

Every subset of the other features is considered. A feature's payout is what the prediction gains when it joins that subset, averaged over all of them.

Nothing goes missing

The efficiency axiom guarantees the contributions add up exactly to the gap between this incident and the average case. That is why the waterfall closes on +7.07 with nothing unexplained.

Fast enough to ship with the alert

Done directly, 38 features mean 238 ≈ 2.7 × 1011 subsets. TreeSHAP walks the trees instead: with 1,200 trees, 63 leaves and depth 7, that is under 3.5 ms per incident.

On the record Every prediction is stored with its full attribution vector, timestamped and signed into an append-only audit log, so a decision can be reconstructed exactly during a post-incident review.

The attribution vector is rendered as a tactical evidence card. The officer ordering a response is a senior administrator, not a data scientist, so the numbers arrive already translated.

04 — mathworks

A flare the size of a car park, seen from 800 km

A refinery flare tip covers about 10 m². A VIIRS pixel covers 140,625 m². The flare occupies seven hundredths of a thousandth of what the sensor is looking at, and yet it sets off every fire detection algorithm in operation, every single day.

That is not a flaw in the algorithms. It is Planck's law. Radiance in the mid-wave infrared rises roughly as the tenth to twelfth power of temperature, so a target at 1,400 K is about 26,000 times brighter there than the ground around it. Mixed by area, a sliver of flame still lifts the whole pixel by 38 K. In the long-wave channel, which scales closer to the fourth power, the same flame moves it by a tenth of a degree.

That gap between the two channels is exactly what fire detection tests look for, which is why industrial heat cannot be filtered out with a better threshold. It has to be understood.

Four laws, and the numbers they produce

The physics is not decoration. It is what tells us a flare is a sub-pixel point source rather than a burning field, and it sets the bands every downstream feature is built from.

LawFormWhat it gives usThe number that matters here
Planck
How much a body at temperature T radiates at each wavelength. Everything else follows from it.At 3.9 µm, going from 300 K to 1,400 K multiplies the radiance by about 26,000.
Wien
Where that radiation peaks. Hotter bodies glow at shorter wavelengths.Earth 300 K → 9.7 µm · kiln 750 K → 3.9 µm · flare 1,400 K → 2.1 µm · explosion 1,800 K → 1.6 µm.
Stefan–Boltzmann
Total power radiated across all wavelengths, which is what fire radiative power ultimately measures.The fourth-power term is why a small, very hot object can outshine a large warm one.
Dozier
What the sensor actually records when a tiny hot target shares a pixel with cool ground.The two terms are mixed by area fraction p, so a 0.0071% flare still dominates the mid-wave channel.

One model, two halves

VahniX does not choose between a tree model and a graph model. Both are branches of a single ensemble: the same 38-number vector enters both, their probability vectors are fused, and the result is calibrated once, at the end. What comes out is one distribution over the six classes.

Why not just one The tree branch is precise on the tabular evidence and cheap enough to run anywhere, but it treats every detection as an isolated row. The graph branch sees the neighbourhood and the history that a row cannot carry. Air-gapped deployments fall back to the tree branch alone, which is why it is exported to ONNX and kept independently deployable.

How attention is computed

Attention weights are not an afterthought added for the explainability view. They are numbers the graph network computes for every edge and every timestep in order to make its prediction at all.

01

Score each neighbour

For node , how relevant is neighbour ? The score uses both node states and the edge's own features, distance and time gap.

02

Turn scores into weights

A softmax over the neighbourhood makes the weights sum to one, so no node can inflate its own influence by having more neighbours.

03

Aggregate

The node's new state is the weighted sum of its neighbours' states. Repeat, and information travels across the neighbourhood.

04

Look back, never forward

In parallel, causal self-attention runs over the cell's own history. The mask makes attending to the future impossible, which matters because at inference time the future does not exist yet.

Why the loss function has to work harder

Genuine emergencies are rarer than one in two thousand detections. A model trained on plain cross-entropy can score above 99% accuracy by never predicting one, because the gradient from millions of easy, correctly-classified flares drowns out the few that matter.

The focusing term shrinks as the model grows confident. At 98% confidence on an easy sample it cuts the gradient by roughly 2,500×; on a hard, still-uncertain emergency at 1% it leaves it essentially intact. Class weights are set from the effective number of samples rather than raw frequency.

Alongside it, a supervised contrastive loss shapes the representation before the classifier ever sees it, pushing flares and explosions apart on the unit hypersphere so that rare classes do not sit against the decision boundary.

Measured on what matters

Raw accuracy is meaningless at this class balance, so it is not used.

  • Macro-F1≥ 0.85every class counts equally, however rare
  • Emergency recall≥ 0.96the miss that cannot be afforded
  • Cohen's κ—agreement beyond what chance would give
  • Brier score—split into reliability, resolution and uncertainty

Validation folds are split by geography, holding out whole H3 regions at a time, so a model cannot score well by memorising one industrial complex and seeing it again in testing.

What happens when inputs go missing

Sentinel-2 cannot see at night, Landsat passes once every eight days, and a new region has no history at all. Rather than fail, the engine drops to a smaller model trained on the features it does have, and says which tier produced the answer.
